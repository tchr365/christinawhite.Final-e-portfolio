Traditional and AI Screencasting Projects

SCREENCAST

This screencast teaches step-by-step how to organize documents in Google Drive using folders and also how to add them to the Starred section for quick and easy access. I used Camtasia to screen record the video and audio narration. In the process of editing this video, I learned how to add a video title, adjust the cursor size and color, box in elements on the screen, zoom in, and add a "thank you for watching" message at the end of the video. Camtasia was very easy to use for this project. I did have to change the graphics card setting on Camtasia so that it would render completely on my computer. It kept getting stuck during that process with the original setting. After this was adjusted, it rendered properly and uploaded to YouTube with no issue.

AI SCREENCAST

This is an AI-produced screencast of the Runway AI platform that is used for video, image, and audio generation. I thought this would be easier than the first traditionally produced screencast project, but it was not. It was challenging finding a platform that would create an AI generated video that was longer than fifteen seconds. I finally had ChatGPT do a screencast video with and without audio. It was not what I had envisioned my project to be. I wanted it to look like someone doing an actual screencast of the Runway application. That is what I was originally trying to produce with Google Studio AI - Omni 1.2 Flash videos. The ChatGPT video with the audio was not acceptable. The AI-generated voice was not clear, so I knew that I was not using that version. ChatGPT provided me with a script for the video, and I used this script to create AI-generated narration on Audiate. I uploaded the video file produced by ChatGPT and the audio file produced by Audiate into Camtasia. I learned how to split some sections of the audio in Camtasia so that it was in sync with the video file.

AI VIDEO APP REFLECTION

As an educator, I see the greatest value of an AI video tool like Runway in the conversations students can have about what a video communicates. A short clip gives students something they can pause, question, and revise. In a middle school science lesson, for example, I would ask students to create a ten-second animation showing how a bee transfers pollen from one flower to another. Before generating the clip, partners would sketch what they expect to see. Afterward, they would compare the animation with a scientific diagram and identify one detail the video shows accurately and one detail a viewer could misunderstand. A bee visiting two flowers might look convincing even if the clip never shows where the pollen goes. Explaining that gap would tell me more about a student’s understanding than the final video itself.

Runway also gives students who find drawing difficult a way to contribute visual ideas. In an English language arts class, partners could create a silent scene and write two narrations for it, one from each character’s perspective. Hearing how the same gesture can take on different meanings would give students a reason to discuss word choice and tone.

One drawback is that repeated attempts can consume class time and generation credits. I would give each group a limited number of attempts and ask them to explain the revision they chose. I would base feedback on the accuracy of their explanation, the choices they made for their audience, and the evidence they used to improve their work.
