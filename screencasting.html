Traditional and AI Screencasting Projects

SCREENCAST

This screencast is called "Organize Your Files in Google Drive." It teaches step-by-step how to organize documents in Google Drive using folders and also how to add them to the Starred section for quick and easy access. I used Camtasia to screen record the video and audio narration. In the process of editing this video, I learned how to add a video title, adjust the cursor size and color, box in elements on the screen, zoom in, and add a "thank you for watching" message at the end of the video. Camtasia was not too difficult to navigate as a first-time user. I reviewed some of the tutorials posted on their website prior to starting this project. After I completed editing the video and then tried to upload it to YouTube, an issue did occur with the rendering process. It kept stalling at a certain point. I learned that I had to change the graphics card setting on Camtasia so that it would render completely on my computer. After this setting was adjusted, it rendered properly and uploaded to YouTube with no issue. Camtasia is a tool that I can see using in future projects for school and work.

AI SCREENCAST

This is an AI-produced screencast of the Runway AI platform. Runway is used for video, image, and audio generation. Based on my previous experiences using AI as a co-designer, I thought this would be an easier screencast to produce than the traditional one I created. It proved to be quite the opposite experience. The biggest challenge was finding a platform that would create an AI-generated video that was longer than fifteen seconds. I finally had ChatGPT generate a screencast video with and without audio. These generations were not exactly what I had in mind. I wanted this project to look like someone doing an actual screencast of the Runway application. This is what I was originally trying to produce with Google Studio AI - Omni 1.2 Flash videos, but that did not work out as planned. There were so many errors in those generated videos. There was no way I could use them, and I did not have the time or credits available to edit them. After reviewing the ChatGPT video with the audio, I determined it was not acceptable. The AI-generated voice narration was not clear. ChatGPT did produce a script for the video, and I used this script to create AI-generated narration on Audiate. It turned out perfectly. I uploaded the video file produced by ChatGPT and the audio file produced by Audiate into Camtasia. I learned how to split some sections of the audio in Camtasia so that it was in sync with the video file. Although this final product is not what I originally envisioned, it did fit the overall project expectations. This process taught me so much about the limitations of the AI platforms that I used, as well as reaffirmed the importance of the human component when working with AI.

AI VIDEO APP REFLECTION

As an educator, I see the greatest value of an AI video tool like Runway in the conversations students can have about what a video communicates. A short clip gives students something they can pause, question - “Does this actually show what we meant?” and revise. In a middle school science lesson, for example, I would ask students to create a ten-second animation showing how a bee transfers pollen from one flower to another. Before generating the clip, partners would sketch what they expect to see. Afterward, they would compare the animation with a scientific diagram and identify one detail the video shows accurately and one detail a viewer could misunderstand. A bee visiting two flowers might look convincing even if the clip never shows where the pollen goes. Explaining what’s visible and what the viewer has to assume would tell me more about a student’s understanding than the finished video alone.

I also appreciate how AI video apps give students who find drawing difficult a way to contribute visual ideas. In an English language arts class, partners could create a silent scene and write two narrations for it, one from each character’s perspective. Hearing how the same gesture can take on different meanings would give students a reason to discuss word choice and tone. For example, the same backward glance might suggest worry in one narration and suspicion in the other. Students could then discuss which words changed their interpretation of the gesture.

One drawback is that repeated attempts can consume class time and generation credits. I would give each group a limited number of attempts and ask them to explain the revision they chose. For example, they could finish this sentence before revising: “We want the viewer to understand _____, so we’re changing _____.” I would base my feedback on the accuracy of their explanation, the choices they made for their audience, and the evidence they used to improve their work.

As an educator, I see AI video apps as a way to help students make their thinking visible. A short video clip can give students a new way to express an idea, a reason to question what they see, and a chance to revise their work with a clear purpose. Whatever app I choose, I need to use it intentionally, starting with what I want students to learn and deciding how creating or discussing a video will help them get there. Its role is to enhance instruction, with the teacher’s guidance, thoughtful questioning, and feedback remaining at the heart of the lesson.
